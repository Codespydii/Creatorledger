A media kit is your sales page. When a brand is deciding between you and three other creators, the kit is often the only thing they have to go on. A good one gets you shortlisted; a stale or vague one gets you ignored. Here is what to put in it.

## Lead with the metric that sells, not the vanity one

Subscriber count is the number creators are proudest of and the one brands care about least. What a sponsor is actually buying is *attention they can measure*. Put these front and centre:

- **Average views per video** — far more honest than total subscribers.
- **Engagement rate** — comments and likes relative to views.
- **Audience demographics** — age, location, and (if you have it) interests. A brand selling to UK 25–34s needs to know that is who watches you.
- **Watch time or average view duration** — proof people actually stay.

If your numbers are modest but your engagement is strong, lead with engagement. A small, loyal, high-converting audience is a premium product — price and present it like one.

## Show the work, not just the stats

Brands want to picture the partnership. Include two or three examples of past sponsorships or integrations — ideally with a result ("drove 4,000 clicks," "sold out the discount code"). If you have never done a paid deal, use your best-performing organic content as a proxy for what an integration would look like.

## Make your offer concrete

End the kit with what you actually sell: your deliverable options and a starting price (or "rates on request" if you prefer to quote per project). The goal is to remove friction. A brand that has to email you to find out what you even offer often just moves on. For how to set those numbers, see [how to price your first brand deal](/blog/how-to-price-your-first-brand-deal).

## Keep it current — automatically

The fatal flaw of most media kits is that they are a PDF someone built once and never updated. By the third month the numbers are wrong, and brands notice.
